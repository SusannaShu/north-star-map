Warm Skin v1 — build plans Palm-sized warm, touch-responsive fluid skin · module one of a soft healthcare companion

The layer stack below is the one thing all the plans share. The plans differ only in how the skin moves — and that choice decides which parts actually matter.

The layer stack

Tap Hold open to freeze the exploded view, or Pressed to see it assembled. It loops on its own: two slow breaths, lift apart, settle, then a press.

  • 7 · Organic fabric topThe only part a hand ever touches, so it decides whether the thing reads as alive or as a gadget. Natural fibre, already owned. Her paper's long-term version is a knitted conductive-yarn skin (layers 7 and 6 become one fabric); v1 stitches the two separately.
  • 6 · Capacitive strips + shield4–6 strips cut from the OurSure silver fabric, sewn down with conductive thread, one ESP32-WROOM touch pin each (GPIO4/13/14/27/32/33). They sense a hand before any force lands (approach, ~3–8 cm) and which strip fires first gives the stroke direction. Underneath, separated by a few mm of felt or fabric: a ground sheet of the ATMOSURE copper-nickel fabric, which stops the warm water and the pump motors from reading as a "hand".
  • 5 · Velostat press zones (2×2)Each Chibitronics sheet is only ~9.5×10 cm, so four of them tile the palm pad as four press zones. Each zone sits between a silver-fabric pad (to an ADC pin) and the shared ground sheet above. Resistance falls under force: press, squeeze, hold and where. Dry, flat, nothing to leak.
  • 4 · McKibben fluid muscles1/8" ID silicone tube inside 1/4" PET braid, zip-tied at both ends. Push water in, the tube swells, the braid turns that swell into a shortening pull. Optional for v1 — see the syringe test in Plan B.
  • 3 · TPU water bladder (two chambers)Heat-welded 0.3 mm TPU, ~5 mm thick, split into two chambers so the two pumps can make a travelling ripple. Holds the warmth, gives the pad the dead weight of a body, and — sealed — turns any press or breath against it into a pressure change the MPRLS can read through a short air-filled tube.
  • 2 · Heater film, 2 × 8 W 5 VTwo 50×50 mm polyimide films stuck to the outside of the bladder floor, each with a KSD301 40 °C cutoff on top and in series. Firmware holds the film at ≤38 °C so the hardware cutoff is only ever the backstop.
  • 1 · ESP32-WROOM brainReads touch, Velostat, bladder pressure and temperature; drives two pumps (one DRV8833) and the heater MOSFET. Her ESP32-WROOM (classic DevKitC, micro-USB, Arduino "ESP32 Dev Module") runs every build step. ESP32-S3 2-pack: arriving, swap in later.

Seven interactions, and what makes each one work

The core is "a patch of skin that syncs its breathing to yours". Every part on the list is there for at least one of these. Possible means the parts list plus owned parts can do it; Possible, with care means it works only if the note is followed.

  • 1 · Approach · Possible

    Sense
    Self-capacitance on the silver strips (ESP32-WROOM touch pins T0–T9; never the boot pins 0/2/12/15). Expect a hand at ~3–8 cm on a palm-sized electrode, less once the ground sheet is underneath.
    Respond
    Lean in: a small slow inflation (2–3 mL) and the heater stepping up, before contact.
    Parts
    OurSure silver fabric, conductive thread, ATMOSURE fabric as ground sheet, ESP32-WROOM. MPR121 only if the native pins are too noisy.
    Watch
    The ESP32-WROOM has no driven-shield channel, so the shield sheet is tied to ground (GND). Longer-range approach (30 cm+) needs a ToF sensor, which is a v2 item.

  • 2 · Touch / tap · Possible

    Sense
    A capacitive spike under 300 ms with a small, short Velostat blip.
    Respond
    A single small pulse — a twitch of attention, not a breath.
    Parts
    Capacitive strips + Velostat zones.

  • 3 · Stroke · Possible

    Sense
    4–6 strips firing in sequence give direction and speed. One undivided electrode cannot tell direction.
    Respond
    A ripple that travels the same way: pump A fills chamber A while pump B drains chamber B, then they swap.
    Parts
    Silver strips; two pumps, one DRV8833 (both channels); two-chamber bladder.

  • 4 · Press / squeeze · Possible

    Sense
    Velostat zone resistance (where, roughly how hard) plus bladder pressure on the MPRLS (how hard, overall).
    Respond
    Firm back: the pumps hold or add volume so the pad pushes back instead of collapsing; one slow breath.
    Parts
    4 Velostat tiles, 4.7 kΩ resistors (owned), MPRLS, sealed bladder.

  • 5 · Hold · Possible

    Sense
    Velostat plus capacitive steady for more than 5 s.
    Respond
    Warmth up to the 36 °C setpoint, then move into breathing mode (7).
    Parts
    As 4, plus the heater chain.

  • 6 · Warmth · Possible, with care

    Sense
    DS18B20s: one on the film, one on the top surface under the fabric (the number that matters), one at the reservoir or ambient, one spare.
    Respond
    PWM on the film to hold the surface at 34–36 °C, with a hard ceiling of 38 °C on the film.
    Parts
    2 × 8 W films, KSD301 40 °C in series on each film, NOYITO AOD4184 module × 2, 4.7 kΩ pull-up.
    Watch
    With the film capped at ~40 °C, the top surface will sit a few degrees cooler through water, Velostat and fabric. Warming ~110 mL of water by 12 °C takes ~5.5 kJ: about 12 min at 8 W with no losses, so plan on 20–30 min of pre-warm with one film. Measure the surface temperature on the first build. If it will not get above 32 °C, move the Velostat under the bladder; water passes a press straight through.

  • 7 · Breathing + entrainment · Possible, with care

    Sense
    The user's breathing as a slow pressure wave in the sealed bladder (MPRLS), with the pad held against the chest or belly. Hand-held on a resting palm, the signal is weak.
    Respond
    Swell in phase with the user, then slowly lead them down toward ~6 breaths/min.
    Parts
    MPRLS on an air-pocket tube, two pumps, two-chamber bladder, syringe-barrel reservoirs.
    Watch
    The pumps limit how big a breath can be: ~80 mL/min at 5 V is ~1.3 mL/s per pump, about 6–7 mL per 5-second inhale. Over the whole 15×15 cm bladder that is only ~0.3 mm. Make a small "breath pocket" (~6×8 cm) as chamber B so the same volume shows as a visible rise, and keep the McKibben for the pull. Full method below.

Breathing entrainment: how the demo shows it

Why a sealed water bladder can read breath

A sealed, liquid-filled pouch pressed against a body turns every small load change into a pressure change. That is the same principle as the air-cushion and bed-bladder sensors used for sleep monitoring, which read both respiration and heartbeat (ballistocardiography). A chest or belly rising under the pad changes the bladder pressure on every breath. A hand resting on a table barely moves, so the palm pose gives almost nothing.

How Shionoiri & Osawa (2025) differ: they circulate water through a 3 mm ID silicone tube (~1 m, on a PP film) and read contact as flow resistance on an SMC PSE573. That sensor is a ceramic, liquid-rated part, so it sits directly in the water line. Contact only registers from ~5 N, where the tube starts to collapse, up to ~7 N, where it fully occludes. They state that weak forces are hard to detect and that pump-rate changes bury small signals. Heat comes from Peltier devices on a small tank (40×40×10 mm), with a thermocouple inside the tube, and water at room temperature +5/+10/+15 °C (24 → ~39 °C). They did not attempt respiration. For breath, their result argues against continuous circulation: a sealed, static bladder sensed while the pump is stopped is the better design. It also confirms that a water skin can be the sensor and the heater at once.

Plumbing the sensor (MPRLS stays dry)

Adafruit describes the MPRLS die as "silicone-gel covered". Neither Adafruit nor the public Honeywell pages rate this variant (MPRLS0025PA00001A) for water; Honeywell's full datasheet is behind a sign-in, so verify, and treat it as dry gas only either way. Run a ~10 cm tube straight up from a dedicated bladder nozzle (or a tee with a stopcock) and leave an air column at the top. Water never reaches the port, and the small air pocket passes pressure faithfully. Keep it short and small, because a big air volume damps the signal. The steel port is only 2.5 mm OD, so 1/8" ID tubing is loose on it. Follow Adafruit's advice: put 3/32" ID tubing on the port and nest that inside the 1/8" line. Range check: 0–25 PSI absolute leaves only about 10 PSI (~70 kPa) above room pressure. That is plenty for a bladder, but the sensor must never go on a McKibben line.

Separating its own breath from the user's

  1. Two chambers, two jobs. Chamber A (big, under the palm/chest contact) is the listening chamber and carries the MPRLS. Chamber B (the small breath pocket) does the visible breathing. During entrainment, pump A is stopped. A stopped peristaltic pump clamps its tube shut, so chamber A's volume is fixed and its pressure changes come from the user, not the machine.
  2. Subtract the known crosstalk. Chamber B swelling still presses on A a little. Calibrate once with nobody touching: run the breath cycle and record A's pressure versus B's commanded volume. In use, subtract that curve. What is left is the user.
  3. Filter by time scale. Breathing is 0.1–0.5 Hz, heartbeat ~1–1.5 Hz, and peristaltic roller ripple several Hz. Low-pass at ~0.7 Hz for breath; band-pass ~0.8–2 Hz if a heartbeat shows up.
  4. Fallback if crosstalk is too big: sense in the pauses. Breathe, hold for 1–2 s at the top and bottom, and update the user's phase estimate only during holds. This is slower to lock, but it is clean.

The demo scenario (60–90 s of video)

  1. Set-up. The user lies back with the pad on the belly or sternum, under one hand or hugged to the chest. The laptop shows a live plot over Wi-Fi with two traces: your breath (MPRLS, chamber A) and its breath (chamber B command).
  2. Arrive (0–15 s). The hand approaches and the pad warms and leans in. On contact, one slow breath.
  3. Listen (15–35 s). The pad goes still and warm. The user's breathing appears on the plot, typically 12–18/min. Firmware estimates rate and phase from peak-to-peak timing.
  4. Match (35–55 s). Chamber B swells in phase with the user, at the user's rate, small and soft. On screen, the two traces lock.
  5. Lead (55 s–3 min). Each cycle, the pad's period stretches by ~3–5%, toward ~6/min, with longer exhales. If the user's rate does not follow within a few cycles, it waits and re-matches rather than dragging ahead.
  6. The proof shot. Both traces walk down together: the user's rate follows the pad's. Cut to a close-up of the fabric rising under the hand, then the plot. That is the attunement claim made visible.

Honest limits: not everyone entrains in one sitting, so film several takes and several people. Cross-check the MPRLS-derived rate against a count or a phone breathing app once, so the plot can be trusted on camera.

Four ways to build it

Same stack, four different answers to "what makes it move". With two 100 mL/min pumps now on the list, Plan B is fully buildable; C and D remain cheap fall-backs.

Plan A · first week after the parts land

Sensing-first: the dry pad + heater

Build layers 7–5 plus the heater on a flat pad with no water at all. Prove that approach, tap, stroke, press and hold are cleanly distinguishable, and that the heater chain holds 36 °C and the cutoff actually trips. Motion goes underneath later without touching any of this.

  • Chibitronics Velostat/Linqstat5 sheets, each ~3.75×4"On the list

    Four sheets tile the 15×15 cm pad as a 2×2 grid of press zones; one is spare. Each zone forms a divider with a 4.7 kΩ resistor into one ADC1 pin. Expect drift and hysteresis: filter it, don't measure force with it.

  • OurSure silver-nylon conductive fabric11.5×12.5"On the list

    One sheet (~29×32 cm, ~930 cm²) covers both the capacitive strips (~225 cm²) and the four Velostat bottom pads (~380 cm²), with a little spare. Cut carefully. Silver-nylon does not solder well: sew or snap the leads.

  • ATMOSURE copper-nickel Faraday fabric + tape44×36"On the list

    The ground sheet. One continuous piece is both the shared top electrode of all four Velostat zones (tied to GND) and the shield that keeps the warm water and pump motors out of the capacitive reading. Leave a few mm of felt or fabric between it and the cap strips, or the approach range collapses. There is plenty left over for spares and v2.

  • Adafruit stainless conductive thread23 mOn the list

    Signal leads only (strips and zones to the header). It is resistive (tens of ohms per metre — verify), so it must never carry heater or pump current.

  • MECCANIXITY 5 V 8 W heater film ×250×50 mm, adhesive, ~1.6 A eachOn the list

    Both films together cover only ~50×100 mm of the 150×150 pad. The water spreads the heat; that is why the bladder is there. Use one film at a time at full power (see the power budget).

  • Fielect KSD301 40 °C normally-closed ×5On the list

    One on each film, in series with that film's MOSFET. Snap discs reset some 10–15 °C below the trip point (verify on this part), so if it trips the heater stays off for a while. That is the reason firmware should hold the film at ≤38 °C and never lean on the disc.

  • DS18B20 waterproof probes ×41 mOn the list

    All four on one data pin with one 4.7 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V (owned). Film, top surface, reservoir/ambient, spare. Do not push a probe through the bladder wall: the 6 mm steel can is a leak point. Tape it to the outside.

  • NOYITO isolated AOD4184 MOSFET module ×2On the listVerify on arrival

    One per heater film. Listed as taking a 3 V or 5 V signal. Bench check: drive it from a 3.3 V pin at 1.6 A load and confirm less than ~0.1 V across the output. The optocoupler is slow, so run heater PWM at a few hundred Hz or slower (a 1 s time-proportioned cycle is fine for heat).

  • 5 V 3 A USB-C supply + USB-C→screw-terminal adapter(5-pack)On the listVerify on arrival

    The Pi supply has a captive USB-C plug, so it needs the female-to-terminal adapter. Check with the multimeter that the terminals read ~5 V under a heater load (that confirms the adapter's CC resistors and the supply agree). Then run the 5 V and GND buses in 22 AWG wire (see Added after review), not Dupont jumpers.

  • NOYITO MPR121 breakoutOn the list · backup

    Twelve more touch channels on I2C (0x5A, no clash with the MPRLS at 0x18). It is used only if the ESP32's own touch pins are too noisy next to pumps and heater, or if the pin budget runs out.

  • HeatnBond Litecurrently out of stockOn the list · optional

    It is insulating: edges only, never across a Velostat contact area or under a capacitive strip. It stops the dry layers sliding and making phantom presses. Stitching does the same job, so drop it if it stays out of stock.

  • ESP32-WROOM · organic fabric · jumper wires · 4.7 kΩ resistors · soldering iron · multimeterAll owned

    Her ESP32-WROOM (classic ESP32-DevKitC, micro-USB) runs every step; Arduino board ESP32 Dev Module, CP2102 or CH340 USB driver if no port shows up. ESP32-S3 2-pack: arriving, swap in later. WROOM pin plan: touch strips on GPIO4/13/14/27/32/33 (T0, T4, T6, T7, T9, T8; never the strapping pins 0/2/12/15) · Velostat pads on ADC1 only, GPIO34/35/36/39 (input-only; ADC2 is dead with Wi-Fi on) · DS18B20 on GPIO25 with one 4.7 kΩ to 3.3 V · I2C (MPRLS 0x18, MPR121 0x5A) on SDA GPIO21 / SCL GPIO22 · heater MOSFET GPIO26 · DRV8833 IN1–IN4 on GPIO16/17/18/19 · no driven shield: the shield sheet goes to GND.

Why this first

  • No water anywhere near the electronics.
  • Gets the gesture classifier (tap / stroke / press / hold / approach) written while the bladder is being welded.
  • Proves the heater cutoff before the heater is ever under water.

What it does not give

  • No movement and no breathing yet.
  • No bladder, so no MPRLS signal and no body weight.

Done when: the serial log cleanly separates approach, tap, stroke-left, stroke-right, press and a 5-second hold, and a deliberately overheated film is cut off by the KSD301.

Plan B · the main line

Hydraulic: two-chamber water bladder, two pumps, optional McKibben

Water does three jobs at once: it carries the warmth, it turns a press or a breath into a pressure the MPRLS can read, and it is the working fluid. Two reversible pumps drive two chambers, which gives a real travelling ripple for stroke and lets one chamber listen while the other breathes.

  • PH PandaHall black TPU fabric, 0.3 mm54" × 1 ydOn the listVerify on arrival

    The bladder. Check whether it is TPU on both faces or TPU-coated on one face; if coated, weld coated face to coated face. Weld test strips first and pull them apart by hand before cutting the real bladder. A yard is enough for many attempts.

  • Mini craft iron 80 W, adjustable + HTVRONT PTFE sheetsOn the list

    TPU welds in a narrow temperature band and sticks to bare metal; PTFE gives a clean release. Note the temperature that gives a seam you can't peel, and reuse it.

  • Bladder ports: luer-to-barb fitting + a welded TPU flangein hand · no 3D printMake

    Changed 14 Oct: no printed nozzle -- the school printer cannot hold a 1.8 mm bore or the barb ridges. The nozzle is the female luer → 1/8" barb fitting already in hand. Cut a 20 mm disc from the same TPU fabric, hole a touch smaller than the fitting's neck, push the barb through, and iron it around the neck at the pouch seal setting with PTFE on both sides; the disc then welds into the bladder panel TPU to TPU. TPU does not fuse to the fitting's own plastic, so the seal is the disc gripping the neck: leak-test every flanged fitting under a squeeze. Barb unions and tees join the lines where needed. Backup: a urethane adhesive such as Aquaseal FD around the neck (see Added after review). Plan three ports: pump A → chamber A, pump B → chamber B, and a dedicated sensor port on A, so the MPRLS needs no tee.

  • FTVOGUE 6 V DC peristaltic pump ×20–100 mL/min, reversible by polarity · 2 ×On the list

    Reversible, so the skin can inhale and exhale. When stopped, the rollers pinch the tube shut, which is exactly what lets chamber A "hold" and listen. At 5 V expect ~80 mL/min (verify), about 6–7 mL per 5 s inhale per pump. That is why chamber B is a small breath pocket. Similar 6 V pumps ship with 3 mm ID × 5 mm OD hose (verify), which pushes onto a 1/8" barb. Running current is listed around 0.1–0.3 A for this class; measure it. Add a 0.1 µF capacitor across each motor's terminals to keep brush noise out of the touch channels.

  • DRV8833 dual motor driver5-packOn the list

    One board drives both pumps (2 × 1.5 A channels, 3–10 V); four boards are spares. VM goes to the 5 V rail, IN pins to 3.3 V GPIO, and the board's SLP/EEP pin is tied high if it is not already pulled up (check the module). Use slow-decay PWM for gentle low-speed breathing.

  • Adafruit MPRLS 0–25 PSI ported3965On the list

    The highest-value part: press, squeeze and the user's breath (and maybe pulse) from one sensor. It goes on an air-pocket tube off the top of chamber A, never wetted, and never on a McKibben line (range is only ~10 PSI above room pressure). The 2.5 mm port needs the 3/32" sleeve (Added after review).

  • Pure silicone tubing 1/8" ID × 1/4" OD5 ftOn the list

    All the plumbing runs, plus the McKibben inner tubes. Five feet is enough for both, but not generous: keep runs short.

  • Alex Tech PET braided sleeving, 1/4" + 1/2"50 ftOn the list

    The 1/4" braid over the 1/4" OD tube is the McKibben actuator. It must be snug at rest; loose braid loses stroke. The 1/2" is cable management. Silicone tube with a 1.6 mm wall may need more pressure to contract than the pump makes. That is exactly what the syringe test answers.

  • Zip ties 4" ×200On the list

    Clamp braid and tube together at both ends of each muscle, and every barb joint. If the braid can slide, nothing shortens.

  • 60 mL luer-lock syringes ×10On the list

    Hand test, fill and purge tool, and the pump reservoirs. A 60 mL barrel with its plunger left free-floating is a closed, air-free reservoir for each pump, so nothing extra needs buying.

  • MEETOOT female luer → 1/8" barb ×10 · red-cap luer plugs ×20On the list

    Luer barbs join the syringes/reservoirs to the 1/8" lines; plugs cap the dead end of each muscle and the fill ports. Two female luers cannot join each other, though, and barbs do not join tube to tube, so the straight/tee couplers and a stopcock in Added after review are still needed.

  • Distilled water, 1 galgroceryBuy locally

    No scale on the pump hose or heater side, and lower conductivity if it ever leaks.

Why it is the main line

  • One material, three functions: heat store, sensor medium, actuator fluid.
  • A sealed bladder reads a breath against the chest, which makes entrainment possible at all.
  • Two chambers = travelling ripple for stroke, and listen-while-breathing for entrainment.
  • Thermal mass: the warmth lingers.

Risks

  • Leaks onto electronics: leak-test with water, squeezed, before any wire goes near it.
  • Trapped air in the chambers damps both the ripple and the pressure signal. Purge with the syringe through a stopcock.
  • Breath amplitude is limited by pump flow, so keep the breath pocket small.
  • The peristaltic pumps whirr. That is acceptable for v1, and the reason for the v2 piezo pump.

Done when, in order: (1) a 60 mL syringe push shows two things: whether a McKibben muscle under the bladder reads as breathing or as a pucker, and whether pushing straight into the breath pocket (volume swell) looks better. Film both. (2) The sealed bladder held against the belly shows breaths on the MPRLS plot. (3) Pumps take over from the syringe.

Plan C · deadline insurance

Tendon drive: a motor pulling threads through the skin

Drop fluid actuation entirely. A micro servo winds low-stretch tendons sewn into the skin, so a breath is an angle sweep. The water bladder stays, sealed and unpumped, as warmth and as the breath sensor. Only needed if the pumped bladder fails to give a visible breath by mid-November. Nothing for it is in the cart, and nothing needs to be.

  • Micro servo (SG90-class) + Dyneema / braided fishing lineonly if neededOnly if needed

    Positional and repeatable: a breath is an angle. A servo needs no driver; a 28BYJ-48 stepper could use a spare DRV8833 from the 5-pack.

  • Tendon guides + anchor patchessilicone-tube offcuts, TPU scrapsFrom Plan B offcuts

    Spread each pull across a patch, which is the difference between a swell and a pucker.

Strengths

  • Motion in an afternoon; no leaks.
  • The sealed bladder still gives warmth and the breath signal, and is even cleaner with no pump in it.

Weaknesses

  • Gear noise; point loads pucker.
  • Steps away from the hydraulic-muscle research line in her paper.
Plan D · a 20-minute experiment, not a build

Pneumatic: the same muscle, driven with air

Fill the syringe with air instead of water and compare the two by hand on the same muscle. This answers the compressibility question with no new parts.

  • Tubing, braid, zip ties, luer fittings, syringeShared with Plan B

    Nothing extra to buy for the hand test. A real air build would need a one-way air pump plus an exhaust valve; buy those only if the hand test wins.

Strengths

  • A leak dries instead of shorting.
  • Fast twitch possible.

Why it is not the main line

  • A squeeze feels mushy.
  • No thermal mass, and the compressible air damps the breath signal.

How they fit together

Plan A while the test welds and flanged fittings are being made. Plan B is the build: syringe test first (muscle vs. volume swell, plus Plan D's air version the same afternoon), then a sealed bladder breath test against the belly, then pumps. Plan C only if no visible breath by mid-November. The demo for 15 Dec is the entrainment scenario above.

Firmware rule: heater duty total ≤ one film (1.6 A). Either run one film at a time, or run both interleaved at ≤50% each and never on together. Drop heater duty to zero while both pumps start. Worst legal case: 1.6 + 0.25 + 2×0.5 ≈ 2.85 A. Add a 470–1000 µF capacitor across the rail at the DRV8833, so pump start-up and heater switching do not brown out the ESP32.

USB logging caution: if the ESP32 is plugged into a laptop by USB and fed from the 5 V rail, the two supplies can back-feed. While logging over USB, power the ESP32 from USB only and share GND with the rail. Or log over Wi-Fi.

Do not buy

PU-coated fabric (must be TPU) · anything 12 V · IRF520 modules, TIP120 or PN2222 for the heater · a second MPRLS for the McKibben line (out of range) · shape-memory alloy wire · HASEL or any high-voltage supply · silicone casting kits (Ecoflex, Dragon Skin, Sil-Poxy).

Safety, non-negotiable

Everything at 5 V from an external supply; no mains near water. Firmware caps the film at 38 °C and the skin surface at 37 °C; the KSD301 40 °C disc is in series with each film as the hardware backstop. Bench-trip it once with a hair dryer before trusting it. The heater sits outside the bladder, never in the water. Fuse the rail. Leak-test the bladder full of water and squeezed, with no electronics attached, before a single wire goes near it. No heater on while nobody is watching during development.

Still open (none block checkout)

  1. Bartels mp6 (v2, silent pump). Bartels' datasheet gives the mp6 ~5–7 mL/min water and ~600–650 mbar max; the sister BP7 is ~9 mL/min, 450 mbar. Its passive check valves make it one-way, so it cannot exhale without a second pump or a valve. At ~1/15 of the peristaltic flow it suits a McKibben muscle better than a volume-swell breath pocket.
  2. MPRLS media rating: confirm from the Honeywell MPR datasheet. The design keeps it dry regardless.
  3. TPU fabric construction (film vs. coated), and whether a TPU flange holds tight on the luer-barb neck. Settled by the first test weld and leak test.

What the build measures for the paper

Every build Day also produces a number the Cuddle Bot paper needs, so a position paper becomes a results paper. The ten measurements are warmth and the 40 °C cutoff, softness, actuation, touch accuracy, breath and entrainment, latency, power, noise, seal life, and a small IRB-reviewed study.
